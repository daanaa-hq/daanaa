# Behavioral Science Brief — Opportunities That Make Giving Natural

**Date:** 2026-08-13  
**Purpose:** Ground Daanaa's `Opportunities` model in behavioral-science evidence while staying within charter and stewardship boundaries.  
**Scope:** Money, time, skills, effort, and retention. Claimed nonprofit pages only. No Daanaa custody of funds.

---

## Product Thesis

Daanaa should not act like a newsletter, data warehouse, or crowdfunding processor.

The better role is:

- help a person understand a nonprofit
- help a nonprofit express current needs clearly
- help the person take the next natural step with money, time, skills, or effort
- give the person a credible reason to come back

Behavioral science supports reducing friction, increasing concreteness, and making the next step legible. It does **not** support manipulative urgency theater, overloaded choice sets, or incentive tricks that crowd out intrinsic motives.

---

## Research Signals

### 1. Identity-congruent asks outperform generic asks

Evidence:
- Kessler & Milkman, *Identity in Charitable Giving* (Management Science, published online 2016; journal 2018) found that identity primes tied to a person's existing donor or community identity increased giving in large field experiments run with the American Red Cross.

Implication for Daanaa:
- opportunities should be framed in the language of the giver's role:
  - `Support local arts in Chicago`
  - `Use your legal skills to help tenants stay housed`
  - `Back general operations for a neighborhood clinic`
- discovery should support identity pathways such as local, cause, alumnus, parent, veteran, profession, or prior helper

Source:
- https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2582

### 2. Concreteness matters, but over-theatrics can backfire

Evidence:
- Rondeau & List, *Matching and Challenge Gifts to Charity* (NBER working paper; published version 2008) found that challenge gifts increased giving, while matching gifts did not reliably outperform in the same way.
- List's NBER summary also reports positive effects from seed money and from announcing a match, but not necessarily from increasing the match ratio beyond 1:1.

Implication for Daanaa:
- concrete, bounded needs can help action
- bigger-sounding leverage ratios are not the core product advantage
- Daanaa should avoid gamified fundraising theatrics and focus on clear need statements

Sources:
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w13728
- https://www.nber.org/reporter/2008number4/using-field-experiments-economics-charity

### 3. Reciprocity decays quickly, so timing matters

Evidence:
- Chuan, Kessler, and Milkman, *Field study of charitable giving reveals that reciprocity decays over time* (PNAS, 2018) found donation likelihood dropped materially as more time elapsed after a service interaction.

Implication for Daanaa:
- if a user has just shown intent, the next step should be available immediately
- nonprofit pages should not hide action behind extra loops
- saved interests, volunteer follow-up, and opportunity reminders should be timely if used at all

Source:
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828571/

### 4. Strong asks can increase action, but they also trigger avoidance

Evidence:
- Andreoni, Rao, and Trachtman, *Avoiding The Ask* (NBER, revised 2016) found verbal solicitation increased giving among those exposed, but also increased avoidance.

Implication for Daanaa:
- avoid aggressive interruption patterns
- no popups, forced modals, or repeated guilt prompts as the main action engine
- action invitations should be visible, not coercive

Source:
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w17648

### 5. Extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation

Evidence:
- Newman & Shen, *Demotivating incentives and motivation crowding out in charitable giving* (PNAS/PMC) found that visually salient thank-you gifts reduced donation rates in their field campaign.

Implication for Daanaa:
- do not center swag, badges, or collectible rewards
- avoid making support feel like a transactional purchase

Source:
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5514700/

### 6. More information helps when it clarifies the recipient, but not all transparency increases giving

Evidence:
- *Charitable giving: The role of framing and information* (2023) found information mattered substantially for a relatively unknown charity in experimental settings.
- She & Sanfey (2023) found people sought more information about preferred charities, but more transparency did not automatically increase donation behavior in their online experiment.

Implication for Daanaa:
- information architecture matters more than information volume
- each opportunity should answer a short set of decision questions clearly:
  - what is needed?
  - who benefits?
  - why now?
  - where does the support go?
  - what is the source of this claim?

Sources:
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10335672/
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10667727/

### 7. Social proof is conditional, not universally positive

Evidence:
- Zhang et al., *Social information decreases giving in late-stage fundraising campaigns* (2022) found some social information can reduce giving in late-stage campaigns.
- Karlan & McConnell, *Hey Look at Me* found public recognition can increase giving, but largely through social-image motives.

Implication for Daanaa:
- avoid simplistic `X people already gave` defaults
- avoid making social proof the core trust device
- if using proof at all, prefer nonprofit-centered signals over competitive donor signals

Sources:
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9714697/
- https://www.nber.org/papers/w17737

### 8. Volunteering intention rises when websites feel usable, trustworthy, and specific

Evidence:
- *Creating compassion: How volunteering websites encourage prosocial behaviour* (Information and Organization, 2019) identified design factors including ease of use, trust, information quality, argument strength, and clear content structure.

Implication for Daanaa:
- volunteer and skilled-help opportunities should be:
  - concrete
  - scannable
  - low-friction
  - trust-labeled
- `what you will do` and `what happens next` should be explicit

Source:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1471772716302160

### 9. Implementation prompts help sometimes, but not universally

Evidence:
- Holland et al. (2006) found implementation intentions helped create new habits in a field setting.
- Carrera et al. (2018) found simple planning prompts did not improve repeated gym behavior in their field experiment.
- Lacetera et al. (2022), *Sign me up!* found a compound-task mechanism improved volunteering-related follow-through in their field experiment.

Implication for Daanaa:
- lightweight next-step planning can help, but should be tested rather than assumed
- examples:
  - `I can help this weekend`
  - `Email me this opportunity`
  - `Save this for payday`
- do not assume a generic planning widget will work on its own

Sources:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103105001356
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167629618301991
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167268122002220

### 10. Retention should come from relevance, not reminders alone

Evidence:
- The reciprocity-decay and volunteering studies above both point in the same direction: timely, relevant follow-through beats generic later reminders.
- The 2025 *Generalizability of choice architecture interventions* review argues effect sizes vary heavily by context, so durable retention should rely on real user value, not assumed universal nudges.

Implication for Daanaa:
- return loops should be built on:
  - saved opportunities
  - local or cause follow-up
  - fresh nonprofit-posted needs
  - progress on interests the user explicitly marked
- avoid newsletter-style retention as the primary mechanism

Source:
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-025-00471-9

## Product Rules Derived From The Research

### Rule 1: Default to unrestricted support

Why:
- flexible giving matches nonprofit reality
- avoids fake specificity
- keeps the action simple

UI expression:
- every claimed nonprofit can publish one default unrestricted support opportunity

### Rule 2: Treat money, time, skills, and in-kind help as equal classes

Why:
- people contribute in different ways
- a giving-first product should not equate support only with cash

UI expression:
- opportunity type filter:
  - donate
  - volunteer
  - skilled help
  - in-kind help

### Rule 3: Make each opportunity concrete

Required fields:
- title
- short need statement
- type
- who benefits
- timeframe
- location or remote
- official destination URL
- source label

### Rule 4: Do not use coercive urgency

Avoid:
- countdown shame
- repeated prompts
- donor feeds
- `everyone is giving, why aren't you?`

### Rule 5: Keep the next step visible and immediate

Use:
- clear CTA
- small number of choices
- immediate save/share/follow-up options

Avoid:
- burying actions after long scrolls
- forced account creation
- dead-end claim flows

### Rule 6: Separate unrestricted from restricted opportunities

`Unrestricted`:
- safe default
- likely Track A implementation candidate

`Restricted`:
- specific program or need
- requires policy before activation

### Rule 7: Preserve agency

Use:
- transparent labels
- visible alternatives
- easy exit

Avoid:
- dark defaults
- false scarcity
- auto-selected donation pressure

### Rule 8: Build retention around return value

Good reasons to come back:
- new opportunities from organizations the user saved
- local or cause-based needs matching prior intent
- volunteer opportunities with upcoming time windows
- updates to nonprofit-posted needs that the user explicitly followed

Bad reasons to come back:
- guilt
- vanity badges
- spammy email pressure
- noisy activity feeds

## Recommended Daanaa Opportunities Model

### Tier 1: Safe to design and likely safe to implement

1. Unrestricted support
2. Volunteer opportunities
3. Skilled-help opportunities
4. In-kind needs
5. Save / follow / return-later states tied to explicit user intent

Constraints:
- claimed organizations only
- off-platform fulfillment
- explicit nonprofit-posted label

### Tier 2: Spec now, governance review before launch

1. Restricted-purpose support opportunities
2. Progress tracking for restricted needs
3. Reminder or re-engagement flows using user intent data beyond clear user expectation
4. Any ranking logic based on opportunity urgency or popularity

## Initial Experiments To Run Later

1. `CTA framing test`
- `Support general operations`
- vs `Support what this nonprofit needs most`

2. `Opportunity taxonomy test`
- four action types visible together
- vs money-only first

3. `Concrete ask test`
- generic volunteer CTA
- vs role-specific volunteer CTA

4. `Source-label test`
- plain opportunity
- vs explicit nonprofit-posted and last-updated label

5. `Return trigger test`
- saved opportunities only
- vs saved opportunities plus explicit follow-up reminders the user opted into

Success metrics should include:
- click-through to nonprofit action destination
- save / return behavior
- volunteer inquiry completion
- claimed nonprofit opportunity publishing rate
- repeat visit rate from explicit saved intent
- user trust/comprehension, not just conversion

---

## Bottom Line

The strongest evidence-backed direction is:

- fewer manipulative fundraising mechanics
- more concrete, provenance-labeled opportunities
- equal respect for money, time, skills, and in-kind effort
- immediate, low-friction next steps
- return value based on relevance and trust
- restricted opportunities only after explicit policy design

Daanaa should make support feel natural, not performative.
