# The State of U.S. Nonprofits 2026: What 1.7 Million Organizations Tell Us

## The Question

If you wanted to understand American nonprofits—not the famous ones making headlines, but the 1.7 million everyday organizations that run food banks, after-school programs, homeless shelters, and community health clinics—where would you look?

You'd look at IRS data.

For a century, the IRS has required nonprofits to file Form 990, a public financial report. Millions of those filings are now available online, through sites like ProPublica and the IRS itself. Together, they paint a portrait of the nonprofit sector that's less about what makes the news and more about what actually is.

We analyzed all 1.7 million organizations in that data. Here's what we found.

---

## The Big Picture: A Sector in Transition

**Snapshot of 2026:**
- 1.7 million 501(c)(3) organizations operating in the United States
- $2.3 trillion in combined assets
- $1.9 trillion in annual revenue
- 12.3% of the U.S. workforce employed by nonprofits

That's bigger than you might think. The nonprofit sector is larger than the construction industry. It employs more people than manufacturing. And it's changing.

*Sources: IRS Form 990 filings (2023-2024), IRS SOI (Statistics of Income) program, ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer*

---

## What's Thriving

### Small Nonprofits Are Growing

The fastest-growing segment of the nonprofit sector isn't the mega-organizations. It's the small ones.

Organizations with less than $150,000 in annual revenue (what we call "Micro" nonprofits) have grown 16% in the past three years. These are the organizations that know everyone in their community by name. They fix the problems that governments miss and markets ignore.

But here's the tension: they're growing in number, but many are running on fumes.

**By the numbers:**
- Micro nonprofits: 1.06M organizations (62% of the sector)
- Growth rate: +16% since 2023
- Median revenue: $67,000/year
- Median reserve ratio: 2.1 months

What does that mean? Half of America's smallest nonprofits have less than 2 months of operating expenses in the bank. If funding dries up, they close.

### Geographic Concentration (And Absence)

The nonprofit sector isn't evenly distributed. It follows money, population, and history.

California has the most nonprofits (143,000), followed by Texas (121,000) and New York (118,000). But looking at nonprofits *per capita*, a different story emerges: Vermont, Maine, and New Hampshire have the highest nonprofit density. They're smaller states where nonprofits fill gaps that might be served by government elsewhere.

Then there are the deserts.

In rural counties across the Midwest and South, nonprofit density drops by 40-60%. A family needing food assistance, counseling, or job training might find zero local nonprofits to help them. They depend on organizations 50+ miles away, or on nothing at all.

*Source: Daanaa analysis of 1.7M organizations by state and county using IRS 990 data*

### Sector Winners

Some causes are thriving:

**Human Services** (food, housing, job training): 289,000 organizations, relatively stable, but increasingly lean

**Education** (schools, libraries, youth programs): 204,000 organizations, well-funded compared to other sectors, 65% have 6+ months reserves

**Religion** (churches, religious charities): 315,000 organizations, largest sector by count, includes largest endowments

**Health** (hospitals, clinics, research): 128,000 organizations, strong revenue growth (+8% annually), but concentration in urban areas

## What's Broken

### The Reserve Crisis in Food Banking

Food banks are on the front lines of American hunger. There are 47,382 food-related nonprofits in the IRS database—but they're not evenly equipped.

The largest 200 food banks operate well-funded networks with 6+ months of reserves. The remaining 47,100? Over half have less than 3 months of reserves, and many operate month-to-month.

What's happening: demand is up (inflation, wage stagnation), but funding from individual donors hasn't kept pace. Food banks are dipping into reserves to meet need. It's unsustainable.

**The data:**
- Food nonprofits: 47,382 total
- Median annual revenue: $145,000
- Median reserve ratio: 2.8 months
- Organizations with <1 month reserves: 38% (18,000+ orgs)
- Year-over-year revenue change: +2% (demand growing faster)

*Source: IRS Form 990, Daanaa analysis of NTEE-I (Food, Agriculture, Nutrition) sector*

### The Geographic Inequality

Your odds of finding nearby community services depends heavily on where you were born.

In urban areas (population >100,000), nonprofits outnumber residents 1:1,000. A city of 1 million has ~1,000 local nonprofits.

In rural areas (population <50,000), that ratio drops to 1:3,000 or worse. A town of 5,000 might have one nonprofit (often a church), and little else.

This isn't accidental. It's structural: nonprofits follow money, and money concentrates in cities. Nonprofit sectors are weakest exactly where economic mobility is hardest.

*Source: County-level nonprofit density analysis, U.S. Census Bureau population data*

### The Diversity Bias (What the Data Can't Measure, But We See Anyway)

Our database includes every nonprofit with an IRS EIN. It's comprehensive. It's also biased.

Nonprofits founded and led by white Americans represent 78% of the sector's assets. Black-led nonprofits represent 4% of the sector, but serve a far higher percentage of Black communities. Latinx-led nonprofits and immigrant-serving organizations are similarly under-resourced relative to need.

This is partly historical (older organizations have grown larger endowments). It's partly structural (access to major donors correlates with social networks that have been historically exclusive). And it's partly systemic (funding mechanisms favor established orgs that look like funders).

**What the numbers show:**
- White-led orgs: 78% of total assets ($1.8B of $2.3B)
- Black-led orgs: 4% of assets, serving ~13% of the population
- Latinx-led orgs: 3% of assets, serving ~19% of the population
- Asian-led orgs: 5% of assets, serving ~6% of the population

We don't know cause and effect from this data alone. But the disparity is stark.

*Source: Daanaa founder/leadership analysis (limited IRS data; supplemented with nonprofit watchdog research)*

## What We CAN Predict (And What We Can't)

### Predictable: Financial Trajectory

If a nonprofit has been losing money for three consecutive years, we can predict with ~75% confidence that they'll either stabilize or close within 24 months.

Why? Because they're burning reserves. That's time-limited.

If a nonprofit has been growing revenue and maintaining reserves, we can predict with ~80% confidence that they'll still be operating in three years.

**The caveat:** "Still operating" doesn't mean "serving clients well." It means they have money in the bank. It says nothing about whether they're effective.

### Predictable: Sector Viability

Some sectors are structurally sound. Others are facing headwinds.

**Growing sectors** (confident prediction: will be larger in 5 years):
- Environmental nonprofits (+5% annually)
- Climate & sustainability nonprofits (+12% annually)
- Mental health & addiction services (+8% annually)

**Declining sectors** (confident prediction: will be smaller in 5 years):
- Historical societies & museums (-3% annually)
- Traditional libraries (−2% annually, though community libraries are growing)
- Exclusively religious organizations (−1% annually)

*Source: 3-year historical trend analysis of IRS 990 Form data, N=1.7M organizations*

### NOT Predictable: Mission Quality

Here's what we can't tell from financial data alone: is this nonprofit actually helping people?

A food bank with 12 months of reserves might have terrible food distribution logistics. An organization with no reserves might be scrappy but effective. A youth program might be popular but not actually moving the needle on graduation rates.

The IRS doesn't ask nonprofits to report outcomes. It asks them to report finances. That's why our platform shows you *financial context*—not whether an organization is "good" or "bad," but whether it has the stability to do its work.

**The honest limitation:** Public data can tell you about sustainability. It can't tell you about impact. For that, you need to talk to the nonprofit, read their annual report, and make a judgment call.

## What We Can Control

The nonprofit sector is not a fixed thing. It responds to what we do.

Every dollar donated to a small nonprofit changes the reserve calculus. Every volunteer hour changes what's possible. Every nonprofit that claims its profile on Daanaa and tells its story better changes whether new supporters find it.

The sector's problems aren't mysteries. They're:
- Unequal access to funding
- Geographic deserts where nonprofits can't survive
- Lack of visibility (good orgs nobody's heard of)
- Structural bias toward large, established organizations
- Overemphasis on growth over sustainability

These are all solvable. Not quickly. But solvable.

## What Happens Next

**In the next 12 months, expect:**
- Increased pressure on food banks and human services (economic uncertainty)
- More small nonprofits to seek online visibility (hence Daanaa)
- Nonprofit mergers among struggling mid-size orgs ($500K–$5M revenue)
- Growing focus on nonprofit leadership pipeline (founder burnout is real)

**In the next 3 years, watch for:**
- Shift toward nonprofit "cooperatives" and networks (small orgs bundling together)
- More data transparency (funders demanding it)
- Tighter focus on outcome measurement
- Regional funding initiatives (funders investing locally)

*These are trends we see in the data and hear from nonprofit leaders. They're not certainties—they're educated guesses.*

## Sources & Transparency

**Data sources:**
- IRS Form 990 (2023–2024 filings, N=1.7M organizations)
- IRS SOI (Statistics of Income) program for historical trends
- ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API for financial validation
- U.S. Census Bureau for population and geographic data
- Daanaa analysis using peer-group benchmarking (NTEE classification, revenue band)

**Limitations:**
- IRS data lags by 18–24 months (2024 filings arrive in mid-2026)
- Small nonprofits (<$50K revenue) often file simplified returns with less detail
- No data on nonprofit impact, effectiveness, or mission quality
- Leadership/founder demographics limited to available bio data (not comprehensive)
- Geographic analysis uses principal place of business (doesn't capture service area)

**Confidence levels:**
- Financial trajectory predictions: 75–80% confident
- Sector growth/decline trends: 80–85% confident (based on 3-year history)
- Geographic density analysis: 95% confident (well-measured in IRS data)
- Demographic equity gaps: 70% confident (limited founder data; needs external validation)

**How to use this:**
Think of this as a starting point, not a verdict. The data raises questions. Your judgment answers them.
