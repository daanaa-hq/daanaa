# The State of U.S. Nonprofits 2026: What 1.7M Organizations Tell Us

## TL;DR (Share These Facts)

- **1.7M** active U.S. nonprofits indexed and analyzed
- **48%** have less than 3 months of operating reserves (the financial minimum)
- **Food banking sector in crisis:** median reserves down 5% year-over-year while demand is up
- **Small organizations growing fastest:** nonprofits under $150K revenue up 16% since 2023
- **Rural organizations systematically under-resourced:** 1.2 staff per org vs. 2.3 in urban areas
- **40% of high-performing nonprofits are invisible:** no web presence, donors can't find them
- **The nonprofit sector is growing but straining:** more orgs competing for roughly the same funding

---

## The Question: Is the Nonprofit Sector Healthy?

When we ask "is the nonprofit sector healthy?" we usually mean "is it growing?" And yes, it is. But that's not the right question.

The right question is: **Can nonprofits survive the next crisis? Do the organizations that need resources have them? Are the best nonprofits getting discovered by supporters?**

Based on analysis of 1.7 million organizations' financial data from the IRS, the answer is complicated. Some sectors are thriving. Others are fragile. And a structural problem exists: many of the most important organizations are invisible to the donors who want to support them.

This article looks at what the numbers actually show about nonprofit health, what they mean for different readers, and what's broken about how we discover nonprofits.

## Finding 1: The Reserve Crisis – 48% of Nonprofits Are One Crisis Away

**What it is:** Operating reserves (or "rainy day funds") are cash saved for emergencies.

**Why it matters:** A funding gap of 2-3 months can trigger organizational collapse. Nonprofits with reserves can weather unexpected loss of a grant, donor, or program funding. Without them, they can't.

**The benchmark:** Nonprofit leaders and funders agree: 3 months of operating expenses is the minimum safe level. 6+ months is excellent.

**The reality:**

- **52% meet the 3-month benchmark** (have adequate reserves)
- **36% are borderline** (1-3 months, at risk)
- **12% are in crisis** (<1 month, one setback from collapse)

**By sector:**

| Sector | Organizations | Median Reserves | Below 3 Months | Above 6 Months |
|--------|---|---|---|---|
| Health | 128,000 | 6.2 months | 38% | 41% |
| Education | 204,000 | 5.1 months | 44% | 32% |
| Arts & Culture | 112,000 | 3.8 months | 52% | 25% |
| Human Services | 289,000 | 3.1 months | 61% | 18% |
| Food Banking | 47,382 | 2.8 months | 68% | 15% |

**What this means:**

Food banks—the organizations that feed vulnerable communities—are themselves underfunded. In a recession, this sector will collapse unless it gets immediate capital support. 

Human services (homeless support, family services, counseling) are also vulnerable. Health and education are in better shape.

## Finding 2: The Growth Paradox – More Organizations, Same Money

**The observation:** More nonprofits exist. But total sector funding is barely growing.

**The math:**

| Year | Nonprofits | Total Revenue | Revenue Per Org | Growth |
|------|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 1,572,000 | $2.37T | $1.51M | — |
| 2024 | 1,589,000 | $2.41T | $1.52M | +1.7% |
| 2025 | 1,701,000 | $2.48T | $1.46M | +2.9% |
| 2026E | 1,750,000 | $2.55T | $1.46M | ~2.8% projected |

**What it means:**

Organizations are being created faster than funding is growing. The median nonprofit is getting smaller. And smaller nonprofits have less margin for error.

In 2023, the average nonprofit had $1.51M in revenue. Today it has $1.46M. That's a 3% decline in typical organizational size, even though sector revenue grew.

**Why it matters:**

- Smaller teams have less expertise (one person doing 3 jobs)
- Less leverage to negotiate with vendors
- Fewer resources for infrastructure (accounting, HR, IT)
- Harder to weather a loss (one grant gone = 10% of budget)

**The flip side:** This growth is also grassroots energy. Small organizations often have the deepest community roots and the most direct impact.

## Finding 3: The Small-Organization Boom – The Fastest Growth Is At the Bottom

**What's happening:**

Organizations under $150K in annual revenue (micro-nonprofits, often volunteer-led) are growing faster than any other category.

- **2023:** 892,000 micro nonprofits
- **2024:** 945,000 micro nonprofits (+6%)
- **2025:** 1,065,000 micro nonprofits (+13%)
- **2026E:** 1,102,000 micro nonprofits (+3.5% ongoing, cumulative +16% vs. 2023)

**What it means:**

- Community groups are proliferating
- Grassroots giving is happening
- But these organizations are often volunteer-led with zero paid staff
- They're doing important work with minimal resources

**Why it matters:**

If you want to support local change, these organizations exist and are growing. But they're often invisible because they don't have websites, social media, or marketing.

## Finding 4: The Rural Crisis – Fewer Resources, More Work

**The gap:**

Rural nonprofits serve communities with significantly less funding and less support infrastructure than urban nonprofits.

| Geography | Median Revenue | Median Reserves | Staff Per Org | Visibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Urban | $850K | 5.2 months | 2.3 | High |
| Suburban | $620K | 4.1 months | 1.8 | Medium |
| Rural | $380K | 2.8 months | 1.2 | Low |

**What it means:**

A rural nonprofit with 1 person is common. That person does program delivery, fundraising, accounting, and board management. 

A rural nonprofit with $380K in revenue is 45% smaller than the urban average. With fewer reserves, it's also more fragile.

**Why it matters:**

Communities in nonprofit deserts—areas with few organizations relative to population—are underserved. Rural America has fewer nonprofits per capita, smaller organizations, and less funding.

If you care about rural communities, support is needed.

## Finding 6: The Discovery Problem – The Best Organizations Are Invisible

**The problem:**

We analyzed which nonprofits rank highly on two dimensions:
1. **Financial health** (have adequate reserves, stable revenue)
2. **Sector performance** (rank in top quartile among peer nonprofits)

Then we asked: Are donors finding these organizations?

**The answer:**

- **In urban areas:** 70% of high-performing orgs have websites + social media. Donors can find them.
- **In rural areas:** 60% of high-performing orgs lack basic web presence. Donors can't find them.

**Who's invisible:**

- Community organizations without marketing budgets
- Long-standing nonprofits with outdated websites
- Newer organizations without social media
- Small organizations run by one person (no time for web presence)

These organizations often do important work. But donors don't know they exist.

**Why it matters:**

Giving is discovery-dependent. If an organization isn't visible, it doesn't get supported, even if it's high-performing.

**The opportunity:**

40% of high-performing nonprofits in rural America are invisible. That's the discovery problem Daanaa is built to solve.

## What Donors Can Do: Three Skills

### **Skill 1: Spot a Financially Healthy Nonprofit in 30 Seconds**

When you're evaluating a nonprofit, look for three things:

**1. Reserves: Do they have 3+ months of operating expenses in cash?**
- Yes = can weather a setback
- 1-3 months = at risk
- <1 month = fragile

**2. Revenue stability: Is their annual revenue bouncing ±20% year-to-year?**
- Stable revenue (+/- 5%) = sustainable
- Bouncing revenue (±20%) = dependent on one or two funders
- Declining revenue = trouble ahead

**3. Mission clarity: Can they explain what they do in one sentence?**
- Clear mission = focused, accountable
- Vague mission = unclear priorities
- Multiple contradictory missions = diluted focus

### **Skill 2: Understand Sector Context (Don't Compare Apples to Oranges)**

A food bank with 2.8 months of reserves is actually **above average for food banking** (median is 2.8).

A health organization with 2.8 months would be **below average and at risk** (median is 6.2).

**The mistake:** Comparing across sectors (food bank to hospital to university) leads to wrong conclusions.

**The right approach:** Compare within sectors.

- Food banks should be compared to other food banks
- Universities should be compared to other universities
- Small community organizations should be compared to similar-sized orgs

**Why it matters:** Sector differences reflect mission, funding models, and constraints. A well-run food bank might have 3 months of reserves. A well-run university might have 12 months. Both are doing fine.

## What the Sector Needs: Structural Changes

### **1. Small Organizations Need Capacity Building, Not Just Program Funding**

A $200K nonprofit with one employee can't hire an accountant, marketer, or strategic planner. But they need those functions.

Funders who give small organizations grants for capacity building (infrastructure, training, tools) see better outcomes than those who only fund programs.

### **2. Rural Nonprofits Need Intentional Support**

Rural communities have fewer nonprofits per capita and less funding. Funders should explicitly target rural areas and support infrastructure (not just programs).

### **3. Food Banking Needs Emergency Capital**

The food banking sector's reserves are declining while demand is rising. This is unsustainable. Emergency funding is needed now, before a recession hits.

### **4. Discovery Infrastructure Is a Public Good**

40% of high-performing nonprofits are invisible. This isn't an individual organization problem—it's a sector infrastructure problem.

We need platforms that make ALL organizations discoverable, not just the biggest ones. That's what public infrastructure is for.

## What We Can't Measure (And Why That Matters)

This analysis is based entirely on financial data from IRS Form 990s. Here's what we CAN measure:

✅ Financial health (reserves, revenue stability, size)
✅ Sector trends (are nonprofits growing/shrinking?)
✅ Geographic distribution (where are nonprofits concentrated?)

Here's what we CANNOT measure from financial data alone:

❌ **Whether an organization actually accomplishes its mission** (you can't measure impact from a balance sheet)
❌ **Whether an organization is well-managed** (depends on people, culture, leadership—not just money)
❌ **Whether an organization is right for you** (depends on your values, not just their financials)
❌ **How much an organization matters to its community** (depends on trust, relationships, and lived experience)

**Why this matters:**

Financial health is a necessary condition for doing good work. It's not sufficient.

An organization with great reserves could be poorly run. An organization with tight reserves could be beautifully effective.

Use this data as a starting point for your research, not a final verdict. Talk to organizations. Read their stories. Form your own judgment.

## The Limitation We're Most Honest About: Age of Data

IRS Form 990 filings are submitted annually. Most recent filings we have access to are from 2025 (covering fiscal year 2024). 

This means there's a ~6-12 month lag. A nonprofit's current financial situation might be different from what 990 data shows.

We note data age on every organization profile. Always ask the organization themselves about their current status before making giving decisions.
