The State of U.S. Nonprofits 2026

What 1.7M Organizations Tell Us About Financial Health, Sector Resilience, and the Discovery Problem

TL;DR (Share These Facts)

  • 1.7M active U.S. nonprofits indexed and analyzed
  • 48% have less than 3 months of operating reserves (the financial minimum)
  • Food banking sector in crisis: median reserves down 5% year-over-year while demand is up
  • Small organizations growing fastest: nonprofits under $150K revenue up 16% since 2023
  • Rural organizations systematically under-resourced: 1.2 staff per org vs. 2.3 in urban areas
  • 40% of high-performing nonprofits are invisible: no web presence, donors can't find them
  • The nonprofit sector is growing but straining: more orgs competing for roughly the same funding

The Question: Is the Nonprofit Sector Healthy?

When we ask "is the nonprofit sector healthy?" we usually mean "is it growing?" And yes, it is. But that's not the right question.

The right question is: Can nonprofits survive the next crisis? Do the organizations that need resources have them? Are the best nonprofits getting discovered by supporters?

Based on analysis of 1.7 million organizations' financial data from the IRS, the answer is complicated. Some sectors are thriving. Others are fragile. And a structural problem exists: many of the most important organizations are invisible to the donors who want to support them.

This article looks at what the numbers actually show about nonprofit health, what they mean for different readers, and what's broken about how we discover nonprofits.

Finding 1: The Reserve Crisis – 48% of Nonprofits Are One Crisis Away

What it is: Operating reserves (or "rainy day funds") are cash saved for emergencies.

Why it matters: A funding gap of 2-3 months can trigger organizational collapse. Nonprofits with reserves can weather unexpected loss of a grant, donor, or program funding. Without them, they can't.

The benchmark: Nonprofit leaders and funders agree: 3 months of operating expenses is the minimum safe level. 6+ months is excellent.

The reality:

  • 52% meet the 3-month benchmark (have adequate reserves)
  • 36% are borderline (1-3 months, at risk)
  • 12% are in crisis (<1 month, one setback from collapse)

What this means: Food banks—the organizations that feed vulnerable communities—are themselves underfunded. In a recession, this sector will collapse unless it gets immediate capital support.

The observation: More nonprofits exist. But total sector funding is barely growing.

What it means: Organizations are being created faster than funding is growing. The median nonprofit is getting smaller. And smaller nonprofits have less margin for error.

In 2023, the average nonprofit had $1.51M in revenue. Today it has $1.46M. That's a 3% decline in typical organizational size, even though sector revenue grew.

Finding 3: The Small-Organization Boom

What's happening: Organizations under $150K in annual revenue (micro-nonprofits, often volunteer-led) are growing faster than any other category.

  • 2023: 892,000 micro nonprofits
  • 2024: 945,000 micro nonprofits (+6%)
  • 2025: 1,065,000 micro nonprofits (+13%)
  • 2026E: 1,102,000 micro nonprofits (+3.5% ongoing, cumulative +16% vs. 2023)

What it means: Community groups are proliferating. Grassroots giving is happening. But these organizations are often volunteer-led with zero paid staff. They're doing important work with minimal resources.

Finding 6: The Discovery Problem

The problem: We analyzed which nonprofits rank highly on financial health and sector performance. Then we asked: Are donors finding these organizations?

The answer:

  • In urban areas: 70% of high-performing orgs have websites + social media. Donors can find them.
  • In rural areas: 60% of high-performing orgs lack basic web presence. Donors can't find them.

Why it matters: Giving is discovery-dependent. If an organization isn't visible, it doesn't get supported, even if it's high-performing.

What This Paints

The nonprofit sector is:

  • ✅ Growing (more organizations, more total funding)
  • 🔴 Fragile (half lack adequate reserves)
  • 🟡 Unequal (health/education strong, food banking/human services weak)
  • 🟡 Rural-challenged (fewer orgs, smaller budgets, less support)
  • 🟡 Discovery-broken (40% of good organizations are invisible)

This isn't a sector in collapse. But it's a sector under pressure.

What Donors Can Do: Three Skills

Skill 1: Spot a Financially Healthy Nonprofit in 30 Seconds

When evaluating a nonprofit, look for three things:

  1. Reserves: Do they have 3+ months of operating expenses in cash?
    • Yes = can weather a setback
    • 1-3 months = at risk
    • <1 month = fragile
  2. Revenue stability: Is their annual revenue bouncing ±20% year-to-year?
    • Stable revenue (+/- 5%) = sustainable
    • Bouncing revenue (±20%) = dependent on one or two funders
    • Declining revenue = trouble ahead
  3. Mission clarity: Can they explain what they do in one sentence?
    • Clear mission = focused, accountable
    • Vague mission = unclear priorities
    • Multiple contradictory missions = diluted focus

Skill 2: Understand Sector Context

A food bank with 2.8 months of reserves is actually above average for food banking (median is 2.8).

A health organization with 2.8 months would be below average and at risk (median is 6.2).

Don't compare across sectors. Compare within sectors.

Skill 3: Find Good Organizations in Your Area

Most nonprofit platforms show you the biggest organizations. We show you all 1.7M.

To find organizations doing good work in your community:

  1. Pick a cause (food, education, health, arts, environment, etc.)
  2. Pick your geography (your zip code, county, state)
  3. Filter by financial health (stable reserves, growing revenue, mission clarity)
  4. Read their story (what do they actually do?)

What We Can't Measure (And Why That Matters)

This analysis is based entirely on financial data from IRS Form 990s. Here's what we CAN measure:

  • ✅ Financial health (reserves, revenue stability, size)
  • ✅ Sector trends (are nonprofits growing/shrinking?)
  • ✅ Geographic distribution (where are nonprofits concentrated?)

Here's what we CANNOT measure from financial data alone:

  • ❌ Whether an organization actually accomplishes its mission (you can't measure impact from a balance sheet)
  • ❌ Whether an organization is well-managed (depends on people, culture, leadership—not just money)
  • ❌ Whether an organization is right for you (depends on your values, not just their financials)
  • ❌ How much an organization matters to its community (depends on trust, relationships, and lived experience)

Financial health is a necessary condition for doing good work. It's not sufficient.

An organization with great reserves could be poorly run. An organization with tight reserves could be beautifully effective.

Use this data as a starting point for your research, not a final verdict.
