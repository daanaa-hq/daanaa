# THE DAANAA RESEARCH AGENDA
Version 1.0 (Living Document)
Drafted 2026-07-11 under founder brief · A twenty-year horizon

## Purpose and Commitments

Daanaa sits on an unusual vantage point: public records for 1.7 million
charitable organizations, peer financial benchmarks across the full range of
organizational size, and a growing longitudinal picture of sector health.
Most research about the nonprofit sector studies the large organizations
that are easy to see. Daanaa's data is strongest exactly where the
literature is thinnest — the small, the local, the quiet.

This agenda is governed by three commitments:

1. **Ecosystem first.** We prioritize questions whose answers benefit the
   whole charitable sector — nonprofits, donors, researchers, and future
   platforms including our competitors — over questions that advantage only
   Daanaa. Per the Foundation's third responsibility, findings are published
   openly so they may inform similar efforts anywhere.
2. **Evidence honestly stated.** Findings carry their limitations. We do
   not dress correlation as cause, samples as populations, or early results
   as settled fact.
3. **Privacy is inviolable.** Research uses public organizational records
   and anonymized aggregates only. No study will ever expose, profile, or
   infer upon an individual's giving.

Questions marked **[start now]** can begin today with data Daanaa already
holds.

## Near Term — Years 1 to 5

The near-term work establishes baselines: careful descriptive research on a
sector that has never been fully mapped at small scale.

**Giving and its barriers.** What actually stops a willing giver — lack of
trust, lack of information, friction, or fear of making a mistake? Where in
the path from intent to gift does generosity most often stall? **[start
now]**: measure which organizational attributes (verified website, clear
mission, financial context) correlate with donor engagement on a neutral,
no-paid-placement directory.

**Nonprofit economics of the small.** What do the finances of healthy
organizations under $150K, under $700K, actually look like — reserves,
revenue mix, cost structure — by cause and by region? How much does survival
depend on funding model (donation-funded, fee-for-service, endowment-funded)
versus management? **[start now]**: publish open peer benchmark tables from
the existing archetype and revenue-band data, so any small nonprofit can see
its financial context for free.

**Trust and information.** Does evidence-based financial context change
giving behavior compared with brand familiarity or marketing polish? What is
the minimum honest signal a donor needs to act with confidence? **[start
now]**: study how data completeness varies with organizational size — the
"data-dark" problem — and quantify how badly conventional ratings
disadvantage small organizations.

**Civil society mapping.** Where are the charitable deserts — communities
whose needs are visibly underserved by organizational presence? How does
nonprofit density relate to population, income, and rurality? **[start
now]**: geographic and NTEE-category coverage analysis across the full
registry.

**Volunteering.** What is the unmet match between people offering skills and
organizations needing them? Which small organizations seek board members and
never find them, and why?

**AI in the sector.** Can local, auditable AI reliably perform the clerical
work that consumes small-nonprofit staff time — summarization,
classification, link verification — and where does it fail? Daanaa's own
pipelines are the first testbed; failure modes will be published alongside
successes.

## Mid Term — Years 5 to 12

The mid-term work turns baselines into longitudinal and causal
understanding, and extends into adjacent systems that shape nonprofit life.

**Longitudinal organizational health.** With a decade of versioned score
snapshots, which early financial patterns predict resilience, decline, or
recovery? Can distress be seen early enough that a community could rally
support before closure — framed always as "needs support," never as
verdict?

**Nonprofit economics.** What is the true administrative overhead imposed on
small organizations by compliance, fundraising, and fragmented tools — and
what does removing a unit of that burden return in mission output? Does the
overhead-ratio fixation measurably harm organizational effectiveness?

**Procurement.** Nonprofits buy insurance, software, supplies, and services
retail while large institutions buy wholesale. How much of the sector's
budget is lost to fragmented purchasing? Can cooperative or transparent
procurement recapture it, and what governance keeps such mechanisms from
capture by vendors? (Any Daanaa role here must honor independence: no vendor
may buy influence over visibility or trust signals.)

**Banking and financial access.** How do small nonprofits actually bank —
fees paid, credit access, reserves held? Are they systematically underserved
by financial institutions, and what product designs would serve them without
extracting from them? Daanaa itself never holds or moves donor funds; this
research informs institutions that lawfully do.

**Behavioral science of sincere giving.** Most behavioral fundraising
research optimizes for extraction — urgency, social pressure, defaults. What
does the opposite look like: interventions that increase giving that donors
reflectively endorse — informed, unpressured, privately made, and unregretted
a year later? This is a genuinely under-studied question, and the one Daanaa
is best positioned to ask.

**Trust infrastructure.** Can trust signals be made portable — evidence-based
context that follows an organization across platforms as a public good
rather than a proprietary rating? What open standards would this require?

**Community resilience.** After disasters or economic shocks, which local
organizations absorb the surge of need, and how does prior visibility and
funding affect their response capacity?

## Long Term — Years 12 to 20

The long-term questions concern the sector's place in society over
generational time. They are stated with humility; the institution that
answers them may look very different from the Daanaa of today.

**Generational transmission of generosity.** How do giving practices pass
between generations, and how do they change as wealth, technology, and
community structure change? Does lowering friction for one generation raise
baseline generosity in the next?

**Civil society and quality of life.** Over decades, what is the
relationship between the health of a community's nonprofit fabric and
measurable well-being — health, education, belonging, mutual trust? This
requires patient data and partnerships with academic institutions; Daanaa's
contribution is the longitudinal organizational record.

**AI and human responsibility in institutions.** After two decades of
machine collaboration, what governance structures actually kept automated
systems accountable, auditable, and mission-aligned — and which safeguards
turned out to be decorative? Daanaa should publish its own record, including
its failures, as a case study.

**The economics of a strengthened sector.** If information barriers,
procurement waste, and administrative burden are substantially reduced, what
does the sector do with the recovered capacity? Does support shift toward
effectiveness, toward proximity, toward neither?

**Institutional longevity itself.** What distinguishes charitable
institutions that improve across generations from those that calcify or
drift? Daanaa's own library, decision logs, and succession record are
deliberately kept as primary sources for this question.

## Method and Governance

Research at Daanaa follows the same constitution as everything else.
Methods are published with findings. Data used is public or anonymized
aggregate, always. Studies involving partners are disclosed; no funder or
partner may shape a finding. Negative and null results are published with
the same care as positive ones — a research program that only reports
successes is marketing. Where Daanaa lacks competence, it partners with
universities and sector researchers rather than overreaching, and it makes
its datasets available to independent scholars under the same privacy
constraints it holds itself to.

Each year, this agenda is reviewed: questions answered are marked and
archived, questions made obsolete are retired with reasons, and new
questions earned by the year's learning are added. A twenty-year agenda is
not a promise of what will be found. It is a promise of what will be asked,
honestly, in the open, for the benefit of everyone doing this work.

The work continues.
