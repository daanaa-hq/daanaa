# GLOBAL PRINCIPLES, LOCAL IMPLEMENTATION
Library Document 010 · Version 0.1 DRAFT — drafted from corpus WITHOUT founder brief; awaiting founder review and brief

## The Third Responsibility

The Foundation assigns Daanaa three responsibilities, in order: to the
organizations and communities it serves; to the preservation of the
institution for future generations; and third, to openly share ideas,
research, and stewardship practices that may inspire similar efforts in
other countries — while respecting their own cultures, laws, and
charitable traditions.

This document gathers what the corpus establishes about that third
responsibility: what travels, what must not be exported, and how an
institution serves the wider world without presuming to lead it. The
charter states the rule in four words: principles universal,
implementation local.

## What the Corpus Claims Is Universal

The corpus grounds its global posture in one observation, made
independently in several documents: generosity is not an invention of any
one society. The practice of setting aside part of what one has for
someone who needs it more appears in every culture we have record of, in
every faith tradition, and in every generation, including those with very
little to spare. The forms differ — a harvest shared, a debt forgiven, an
hour spent teaching a child, a stranger sheltered — but the impulse is the
same. It is part of what people are.

From this the corpus derives the claims it treats as candidates for
universality: that people will give, whatever the era; that need will
exist, because no plausible society meets every human need through markets
and governments alone; that into that gap, people everywhere organize
themselves to respond, whatever such organizations are called; that trust
is earned continuously or decays, in every culture, because it is a
relationship between people and not a property of systems; and that
compassion remains human — a machine may remove every obstacle between
giver and receiver, but it cannot care.

Two honesty notes bind these claims. First, per the maturation discipline
of Amendment 1, they are one generation's convictions until repeated
evidence and long usefulness earn them the Universal Principle
classification; eloquence does not confer status. Second, the charter's
instruction on method: compare ideas from many cultures and civilizations,
and seek universal wisdom rather than cultural dominance. A principle is
not universal because Daanaa holds it. It is universal only if it would be
recognized by people who have never heard of Daanaa — which is the test
future stewards should apply.

## What Is Local, and Must Remain So

Nearly everything else. Daanaa serves the United States, and the honest
inventory of its local particulars is long: a legal form of charity
defined by one country's tax code; public records produced by one
country's agencies; a scoring methodology built on those records; a
national registry; an English-language product; and assumptions about
regulators, banks, filing cycles, and donor habits that quietly pervade
every implementation choice. None of this should travel. A charitable
tradition is not an inefficiency to be standardized; it is part of how a
community takes responsibility for itself. That is not a limitation of
civil society. It is its strength, everywhere.

The corpus states the boundary as an instruction to every future steward:
never assume another country should copy Daanaa. Encourage local
implementation respecting culture, law, language, charitable traditions,
governance, and history. What a trust signal looks like, what privacy
requires, what counts as a charity, how verification can work, who may
lawfully do what — these answers belong to the people who live inside
them.

## Inspiration, Not Replication

The corpus chooses its verb carefully: efforts elsewhere are to be
*inspired*, not franchised, licensed, or operated. The distinction is
structural, and it follows from principles the institution already holds.

An institution that operated giving infrastructure worldwide would become
the kind of concentration the corpus warns against everywhere else:
technology gathering around a single powerful actor, a mission inverted
into a toll booth, dependence without capacity. The charter's instruction
to avoid becoming indispensable applies between institutions and countries
exactly as it applies between Daanaa and a small nonprofit. Applied
globally, the Capacity Transfer question becomes: what can another
country's civil society now accomplish independently that it could not
before? If the answer is "run Daanaa's software under license," the answer
is dependence. If the answer is "build their own, faster and wiser,
because our reasoning was public," the answer is capacity.

So the export is the record, not the product. What Daanaa can honestly
offer the world is what the corpus already commits to producing: methods
published; research released openly, with negative and null results
carried at the same weight as positive ones; decisions recorded with their
reasons; principles amended in the open, with a revision log that denies
drift its silence; failures documented as case studies — including,
explicitly, the long-term record of what actually kept automated systems
accountable and which safeguards turned out to be decorative. The research
agenda's first commitment is ecosystem first: questions whose answers
benefit the whole sector, including future platforms and competitors,
anywhere. An institution's library, offered freely, is a gift that
respects the recipient's sovereignty. An institution's franchise is not.

The mutual-respect article completes the posture: Daanaa serves people of
every culture, faith, and worldview, and borrows wisdom from all of them
without belonging to any. The borrowing runs inward as well as outward —
the corpus expects Daanaa to learn from other charitable traditions, not
merely to publish at them. Even the institution's name carries this
inheritance: *daanaa* descends from a word for giving that predates every
system it now names.

## Open Questions

The corpus establishes the posture firmly; it is silent on most of the
mechanics. Rather than invent doctrine, this draft records the silences.

**Open Question — the boundary cases.** Between "publish openly" and
"never operate abroad" lies a wide middle: answering questions from a
group in another country, reviewing their draft principles, co-authoring
research, accepting their contributions to ours. Which of these are
inspiration and which are the beginning of operation? No document draws
the line.

**Open Question — language.** A library offered for worldwide adaptation
is currently written in one language. Is translation part of the third
responsibility, and if so, who stewards fidelity of meaning — since a
mistranslated principle is a diluted principle, and dilution is forbidden
even when accidental?

**Open Question — the name.** If others adapt the principles, may they
call the result Daanaa? The corpus never says whether the name is offered
along with the wisdom, or whether independence is better served by every
implementation carrying its own name. The steward's reading of the
corpus's logic favors local names for local institutions — but that is
inference, not doctrine, and the founder should decide.

**Open Question — reciprocity.** When another country's implementation
learns something ours should adopt, what is the path by which their lesson
amends our library? Amendment processes exist for internal change; nothing
describes inbound wisdom from an external adaptation.

**Open Question — divergence.** If an effort elsewhere adopts the
principles and then dilutes them — keeping the vocabulary of stewardship
while ranking, shaming, or selling influence — does Daanaa owe the public
anything: silence, distance, correction? An institution that never
operates abroad has no enforcement power, and perhaps that is the answer;
but the corpus does not say so.

## The Long View

The letter to 2126 closes with the sentence that makes this document's
case better than any argument: someone near you wants to help, and someone
near you needs it, and something stands between them that does not have to.
That sentence is true in every country, in every language, in every
charitable tradition — which is precisely why the response to it cannot be
one institution's product, and must instead be every community's own work,
aided by whatever wisdom travels well.

Principles universal. Implementation local. The work continues — here, and
anywhere.
