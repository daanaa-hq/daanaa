# INFORMATION STEWARDSHIP
Library Document 005 · Version 1.0 (Living Document)
Drafted 2026-07-11 under founder brief

## Held in Stewardship, Not Owned

Daanaa describes more than a million organizations. Almost none of them
asked to be described. Behind every record is a real organization, real
people, and a reputation that took years to build and could be damaged in an
afternoon by a careless fact.

That is why Daanaa holds information in stewardship rather than owning it.
Ownership asks "what may we do with this?" Stewardship asks "what were we
entrusted with this for?" The information in our systems was entrusted to
us, by public agencies, by the organizations themselves, and by the simple
fact of describing others, for one purpose: to help people give well and to
help organizations be known truthfully. Every rule in this framework follows
from that purpose. This document elaborates Article III of the Stewardship
Constitution: information about others carries responsibility toward them.

## The First Law: Everything Has Provenance

No piece of information enters, moves through, or leaves Daanaa's systems
without an answer to the question: where did this come from, and how sure
are we? Provenance is not metadata added later. It is part of the fact
itself. A number without a source is not a fact; it is a rumor with good
posture.

Provenance never washes out. Summarizing, aggregating, reformatting, or
repeating a piece of information does not upgrade its certainty. The
weakest source in a chain sets the ceiling for everything derived from it.

## The Eight Provenance Classes

Every piece of information Daanaa holds belongs to exactly one of eight
classes. The classes are ordered roughly by the kind of trust each deserves,
though none is "better" than another; each is honest about what it is.

**1. Public record.** Produced by a government or official body in the
course of its duties: an IRS registration, a Form 990 filing, a revocation
list. Public records are authoritative for what they state, but they are
dated; a 990 describes a fiscal year that may be two years past. So public
records always carry their filing period, and we already disclose the
filing year alongside financial figures drawn from them, because a true
number from 2022 presented as current is a falsehood by omission.

**2. Nonprofit supplied.** Stated by the organization about itself: a
mission written by its founder, a program description, a correction. The
organization is the authority on its own intentions, so this class is
displayed with attribution and taken at its word about matters of intent,
while matters of external fact remain checkable against other classes.

**3. Verified.** Confirmed through a defined verification procedure by a
human with standing to confirm it. When an organization claims its record,
each attestation is captured and versioned, so that what was verified, by
whom, and when is permanently reconstructable. Verification is an event
with a date, not a permanent halo; verified information ages back toward
"needs re-verification."

**4. Research.** Findings from Daanaa's own studies or from external
scholarship: sector patterns, methodology analyses, survey results. Research
carries its method with it. A finding whose method cannot be described is
not research; it is opinion wearing a lab coat, and belongs in class eight.

**5. AI generated.** Produced by an AI system: a drafted mission summary
for an organization that never wrote one, a donation link discovered by
automated search. This class is always labeled, at the level of the
individual fact. In our systems today, every mission records whether it was
AI-written or scraped from the organization, and an AI-found donation link
is marked as beta until the organization verifies it, at which point it
graduates to class three. AI-generated information that cannot be verified
is discarded, never displayed; unlabeled AI output is a provenance
violation, full stop.

**6. Calculated.** Derived deterministically from other information by a
published method: a peer financial context score, a percentile within a
peer group. A calculation is only as honest as two disclosures: its inputs
(with their provenance) and its method (with its version). Daanaa snapshots
its scores and versions its scoring methodology, so any published score can
be traced to the exact method and data that produced it, years later.

**7. Estimated.** Derived by a method that involves judgment, sampling, or
inference: a coverage estimate, an approximate figure where the exact one
is unavailable. Estimates are stated as estimates, with their basis. An
estimate presented as a measurement is the most common lie in data work,
and it is banned here.

**8. Human opinion.** A judgment by a person: an editorial framing, a
recommendation of emphasis, an interpretation. Opinion is legitimate and
sometimes the most valuable class of all, provided it is attributed and
never dressed as any of the other seven.

## Lifecycle: Information Ages Like Everything Else

Every piece of information has a life: it is gathered, used, ages, and is
either refreshed, retired, or preserved. Stewardship means managing that
whole life, not just the moment of collection.

**Gathering.** Information is collected only for the mission, from the most
authoritative source available, with provenance recorded at entry.

**Freshness.** Every class decays at its own rate. A public record decays
with filing cycles; a verified fact decays as the verifying person moves
on; an AI-generated draft decays fastest of all. Displayed information
carries its date wherever the date changes its meaning.

**Refresh or retire.** When fresher authoritative data arrives, it
supersedes; the superseded version is not deleted but archived, because the
history of what we believed, and when, is part of the record. Information
that no longer serves the mission and carries risk to the people it
describes is retired.

**Preservation.** Some information must outlive every system that holds it:
the record of our methods, our corrections, our decisions, and the versioned
history of what we published. Preservation means formats that will open in
fifty years, copies that survive any single machine, and documentation
sufficient for a stranger to reconstruct the archive's meaning. An
institution built for future generations does not store its memory in
formats owned by companies that may not exist then.

## Corrections: The Test of the Whole Framework

Any framework can describe being right. Stewardship is proven by how an
institution behaves when it is wrong.

Every organization Daanaa describes has a visible path, on its own page, to
challenge what we say. Corrections are handled without defensiveness: the
record is fixed promptly, the fix is documented rather than hidden, and the
cause is examined so the same error becomes rarer. The corrected version
notes that a correction occurred; silently overwriting an error treats the
people who relied on the old version as if they did not matter.

We treat a healthy flow of small corrections as a sign of trust, not of
failure. The organization that bothers to correct us believes we will
listen.

## Transparency: The Record Explains Itself

Anyone affected by our information is entitled to understand it. That means:
methods published; provenance classes visible in the product, not buried in
policy; significant data decisions recorded with reasons; and known
limitations stated plainly where the data appears, not in a disclaimer three
clicks away. Where evidence is weak, incomplete, or old, we say so at the
point of use. An institution that inflates its certainty spends trust it has
not earned, and information stewardship is, in the end, a discipline of
never spending what belongs to someone else.

## Equal Care

The smallest organization's record receives the same diligence as the
largest's. Data-thin organizations, and there are hundreds of thousands, are
described in terms of what is unknown rather than penalized for it. "We
have limited public information about this organization" is a truthful
sentence; a low score built on missing data is not.

## The Steward's Summary

Eight questions, asked of every fact, forever:

1. Where did this come from? (class)
2. How was it obtained or derived? (method)
3. When was it true? (date)
4. How certain is it? (stated plainly)
5. Who can challenge it? (corrections path)
6. When does it expire? (lifecycle)
7. What happens to its history? (preservation)
8. Whom does it affect, and have we been fair to them? (dignity)

Information handled this way is slower to publish and heavier to maintain.
That is the cost of holding other people's reputations in your hands, and
it is not negotiable.
