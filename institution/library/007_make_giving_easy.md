# MAKE GIVING EASY
Library Document 007 · Version 0.1 DRAFT — drafted from corpus WITHOUT founder brief; awaiting founder review and brief

## The Mission in Three Words

Daanaa's mission is Make Giving Easy. This document gathers what the
corpus already establishes about those three words — what giving means
here, what "easy" means and does not mean — so the mission has one home
rather than living scattered across ten documents. Nothing here is new
doctrine; where the corpus is silent, this document says so, as an open
question, rather than inventing an answer.

## Giving, in the Fullest Sense

Giving is not a synonym for donating. The Foundation names eleven forms:
financial support, volunteering, knowledge, mentorship, professional
expertise, community leadership, advocacy, procurement savings, research,
collaboration, innovation. A retired accountant reviewing a small
organization's books is giving. A board member found for a struggling board
is a gift that may outlast any check. Savings found in a fragmented
purchasing landscape and passed along to an organization are giving. So is
research published freely where only paid consulting existed before.

This breadth is load-bearing. It means every barrier to any of these
forms is within Daanaa's concern, and that the mission cannot be
satisfied — or measured — by donation volume alone. An institution that
made donating frictionless while volunteering, board service, and
knowledge-sharing stayed hard would have served a fraction of its mission
and might not notice.

The impulse behind all these forms is the same, and it is old. Generosity
appears in every culture we have record of, every faith tradition, every
generation, including those with very little to spare. The desire to help
does not need to be manufactured. It exists, everywhere, in abundance. It
needs to be unobstructed.

## The Barriers

Between the person who wishes to help and the organization doing the work,
barriers accumulate. The corpus names them plainly: limited visibility,
administrative burden, fragmented information, unnecessary costs, lack of
trust, and unequal access to technology.

Each barrier has a face. The organization cannot be found — a food pantry
serving its county for twenty years is invisible to a donor two towns
away. Its record cannot be verified — so the donor, uncertain, hesitates,
and the hesitation hardens into inaction. Its needs are not known —
willing hands exist, but the matching is broken. The tools that would make
it visible and trustworthy are priced for institutions a thousand times
its size. And the administrative weight of simply existing falls on a
founder with no one to delegate it to, taking hours directly from the
mission.

The mission's most precise formulation may be the sentence in Document 002:
the generosity that was ready is never expressed. That is the loss Daanaa
exists to prevent. It is invisible in every ledger — no one records the
gift that didn't happen — which is why an institution must be deliberately
built to see it.

## Friction Is the Enemy — With One Distinction

The corpus is emphatic: point technology at friction, not at persuasion.
Tools that remove barriers strengthen generosity. Tools that manufacture
emotion, urgency, or social pressure to extract a gift corrode it, even
when the cause is good. A gift a donor regrets, or was maneuvered into,
spends the sector's trust to meet one quarter's target.

"Easy," therefore, means unobstructed, not accelerated. Daanaa removes the
barriers between a sincere intention and its expression; it does not
manufacture intentions, hurry them, or grease a path someone did not
choose to walk. The corpus bans the whole toolkit of ease-toward-
extraction: countdown timers, manufactured scarcity, social comparison,
engineered urgency, giving turned into public performance. A person using
a Daanaa system should feel their pulse slow, not quicken.

This reveals a distinction the corpus practices everywhere but has not
named in one place: not all friction is the enemy. Daanaa deliberately
*adds* friction where trust requires it. Unverified donation links are
withheld. AI-drafted text waits for review and wears a label. Scores that
cannot be computed from evidence are absent rather than approximated.
Fail-closed is friction, chosen on purpose. Call the two kinds obstructive
and protective friction: the first stands between a sincere giver and a
real organization and must be fought; the second stands between the
institution and an unearned claim, and must be kept. Making giving easy
means removing the first relentlessly and never confusing it with the
second.

**Open Question:** the corpus does not yet provide a test for telling the
two apart in hard cases. Verification steps protect trust but also slow an
organization's visibility; a claim flow protects accuracy but costs a
founder's evening. When does protective friction quietly become
obstructive? Who decides, and against what standard?

## Ease for Both Sides

The mission is usually read from the giver's side, but the corpus insists
on both. Every hour of administrative weight removed from a nonprofit
founder is giving made easy on the receiving side — the organization
becomes easier to give *to*, easier to trust, easier to find, and its
people are returned to the work the gifts were for. The concierge call
that completes in ten minutes what would have taken an evening of forms is
mission work, not customer service. The eleven capacity dimensions of
Document 006 are, from this angle, eleven kinds of ease.

And ease must compound rather than create dependence. The Capacity
Transfer Principle (an Emerging Principle, not yet constitutional) states
the trajectory: every interaction should leave an organization more
capable than before, so that giving gets easier because the sector is
stronger — not because everyone has become reliant on one intermediary.
An "easy" that must be rented forever from Daanaa would be a toll booth
wearing the mission's name. The corpus is unambiguous that Daanaa never
stands between the gift and its receiver, never handles funds, and must
never become indispensable. Ease is given away, like everything else here.

## What Easy Is Not

For future stewards, the boundaries the corpus has already drawn:

Easy is not persuasive. The desire to help is not manufactured here.

Easy is not fast at trust's expense. Fail-closed friction is kept.

Easy is not public. The decision to give is intimate; ease never means
exposure, social proof, or performance.

Easy is not centralized. If making giving easy ever requires all giving to
pass through Daanaa, the mission has been inverted.

Easy is not free of judgment — it is free of *our* judgment. Daanaa
provides context, not verdicts; the giver's own reasons remain sovereign,
including reasons no recommendation engine would have chosen.

## Measuring Ease, Carefully

The corpus warns that the moment a number becomes a target it begins to
lie, and no number is more corruptible than one measuring "ease of
giving" — the extraction industry measures exactly that, ruthlessly.
Daanaa's version must measure removed obstruction, not induced action:
barriers eliminated, hours returned, organizations made findable, matches
made, capacity retained. The research agenda's framing is the right one —
studying giving that donors reflectively endorse, unpressured and
unregretted a year later.

**Open Question:** the corpus defines what not to measure better than what
to measure. What is the honest, non-corruptible measure of "giving made
easier" — for a donor, a volunteer, an organization — and who stewards it?

**Open Question:** does the mission extend to making giving easy between
organizations — the collaboration dimension of 006 — with the same weight
as person-to-organization giving? The corpus gestures at it, never ranks
it.

## The Standing Test

Every feature, service, partnership, and research project answers the
Foundation's question: does this make it easier for people and
organizations to improve the lives of others? A capability that cannot
answer is not built, however impressive. A barrier left standing because
removing it was unprofitable is a product failure by this institution's
own definition.

The mission fits in three words so that no one can misremember it. The
work of a century fits behind them.

The work continues.
